Music Theory

Learn why notes and chords work together, then try it yourself. Start with intervals, build scales and triads, and discover the chords in a key.

Using your current learning key and mode

Intervals: the distance between notes

An interval is simply the distance between two notes. The easiest way to measure that distance on guitar or bass is to count frets on the same string.

Half step = move 1 fret. If you play the 3rd fret, move to the 4th fret on the same string. That is one half step.
Whole step = move 2 frets. If you play the 3rd fret, move to the 5th fret on the same string. That is one whole step.
For example, E to F is a half step; C to D is a whole step. You may also hear “semitone” for half step and “tone” for whole step.

Scales are interval recipes

Scales are built by moving between notes in a pattern. Whole step (W) means move 2 frets on the same string. Half step (H) means move 1 fret. For example, starting at the 3rd fret, a whole step takes you to the 5th fret; a half step takes you to the 4th fret.

A major scale uses W – W – H – W – W – W – H. Just follow those fret distances in order. Natural minor uses W – H – W – W – H – W – W.

Modes change the intervals measured from the root, changing which notes create tension, brightness, or a darker sound. The same seven notes can also make different modes when a different note feels like home. Explore each mode below to see what changes and when you might use it.

The displayed note spellings use sharps for enharmonic pitches. In written theory, some keys are more naturally spelled with flats.

Mode Study — seven ways to hear C as home

Every example below starts on C. These are parallel modes: the root stays the same while the interval recipe changes. Compare them to C major (C–D–E–F–G–A–B) or C natural minor (C–D–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭). You can explore other roots with the controls above.

C Ionian (major) · 1–2–3–4–5–6–7
Notes: C–D–E–F–G–A–B
Why it sounds this way: Its major 3rd (E) gives a major character, and B is only a half step below C, creating a strong pull home.
Try it: Over a C major chord or a C–F–G progression in pop, folk, or worship.
C Dorian · 1–2–♭3–4–5–6–♭7
Notes: C–D–E♭–F–G–A–B♭
What changes: Compared with C natural minor, A replaces A♭: the raised 6th is its signature.
Why it works: E♭ keeps the minor quality, while A adds a brighter lift and makes an F major (IV) chord possible.
Try it: Over Cm–F, or a minor funk/rock groove emphasizing A.
C Phrygian · 1–♭2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7
Notes: C–D♭–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭
What changes: C natural minor's D becomes D♭ (♭2).
Why it works: D♭ is just a half step above C, creating immediate tension when it moves toward the root.
Try it: In a heavy C-centered riff alternating C and D♭.
C Lydian · 1–2–3–♯4–5–6–7
Notes: C–D–E–F♯–G–A–B
What changes: C major's F becomes F♯ (♯4).
Why it works: The raised 4th avoids the ordinary F–E half-step pull and adds a floating, open tension against C.
Try it: Over Cmaj7 with D major or a sustained F♯ melody note.
C Mixolydian · 1–2–3–4–5–6–♭7
Notes: C–D–E–F–G–A–B♭
What changes: C major's B becomes B♭ (♭7).
Why it works: B normally sits a half step below C and pulls strongly home. B♭ sits a whole step below, softening that pull while E keeps the major sound.
Try it: Over C7, or a C–B♭–F rock progression.
C Aeolian (natural minor) · 1–2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7
Notes: C–D–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭
Why it sounds this way: E♭ creates the minor quality; A♭ adds a darker color, and B♭ lacks the leading-tone pull of B natural.
Try it: Over Cm–A♭–B♭ in a minor-key song.
C Locrian · 1–♭2–♭3–4–♭5–♭6–♭7
Notes: C–D♭–E♭–F–G♭–A♭–B♭
What changes: Compared with C natural minor, D becomes D♭ (♭2) and G becomes G♭ (♭5).
Why it works: The flattened fifth removes the stable perfect fifth of a minor chord, creating a diminished tonic and a deliberately unsettled sound.
Try it: Over Cm7♭5 in a jazz passage or an intentionally tense riff.

Parallel vs. relative modes: C Dorian and C Mixolydian share the same root but use different notes (parallel). By contrast, C Ionian and D Dorian contain the same seven notes of C major, but sound different when C or D is treated as home (relative). A mode's sound depends on the musical center and supporting chords, not just playing a familiar shape.

Build a triad: root, third, fifth

A triad is a three-note chord built from a root (1), a third (3), and a fifth (5). The root names the chord; the third helps determine whether it sounds major or minor; lowering the fifth creates a diminished triad.

Triad recipes (measured from the chord's own root)
Major: 1–3–5 · bright or settled
Minor: 1–♭3–5 · darker
Diminished: 1–♭3–♭5 · tense or unsettled
Augmented: 1–3–♯5 · an additional quality you can explore below

Start with C major: C–D–E–F–G–A–B. Take C (root), skip D, take E (third), skip F, and take G (fifth). C–E–G = C major.

Now start on D, but stay in the key of C major. A D major triad would be D–F♯–A, but F♯ is not in the C major scale. Lowering its third to F gives D–F–A = D minor. The key determines which notes are available, so different starting notes naturally produce different chord qualities.

The easier method: stay inside the parent scale.
Write the seven notes of your key. Start on the note you want as the chord root, then take every other note, wrapping around the scale if needed. In C major, starting at D: D–E–F–G–A = D–F–A (D minor). Starting at B gives B–D–F, a diminished triad. You don't need to build a separate major chord first and adjust it.

Why the chord pattern repeats: A seven-note scale has a fixed sequence of whole and half steps. Stacking every other note produces different distances between each root, third, and fifth. Those distances make each triad major, minor, or diminished.

Diatonic triads in a major key
I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii°
Major – minor – minor – Major – Major – minor – diminished
In C major: C – Dm – Em – F – G – Am – Bdim.
Diatonic triads in a natural minor key
i–ii°–III–iv–v–VI–VII
minor – diminished – Major – minor – minor – Major – Major
In A natural minor: Am – Bdim – C – Dm – Em – F – G.

Reading Roman numerals: Uppercase (I, IV, V) means a major triad; lowercase (ii, iii, vi) means minor; the small circle (°) means diminished. The number tells you which scale degree is the chord's root.

Important: The minor-key pattern above is for natural minor. Harmonic minor raises the seventh scale degree, so some chords change (for example, in A harmonic minor the E chord can become E major rather than E minor).

Try it: Select D and Minor below to hear the D–F–A relationship on paper, then switch to Major to see F change to F♯. The interactive builder still works for any root and chord quality.

Try changing only the chord quality and watch which note moves. That's why the third matters so much.

Chords that belong to a key

Build a triad on each note of a seven-note scale by taking every other scale note: R–3–5 from each starting scale note, then the next scale note, and so on. The chord qualities emerge from the scale's intervals.

Pentatonic scales contain five notes, so they don't generate the same seven diatonic triads. Choose a seven-note mode here.
